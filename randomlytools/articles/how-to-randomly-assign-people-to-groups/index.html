How to Randomly Assign People to Groups

Random group assignment is one of the simplest ways to create teams without spending a long time deciding who should work together. It can be useful for classrooms, workshops, office activities, sports practice, parties, games, networking events, and community activities.

The goal is usually simple: every eligible participant should have a fair chance of being placed in any group, while the final groups remain practical to use.

Quick method

  1. Make one clean list of participants.
  2. Remove duplicates and anyone who is not eligible.
  3. Choose the number of groups or the desired group size.
  4. Randomize the list.
  5. Distribute names across groups.
  6. Check only for necessary practical issues such as an absent participant.

Choose group count or group size

When you know the number of groups

If 24 participants need to become four groups, divide the randomized list into four groups of six. This approach works well when the activity is designed around a fixed number of teams.

When you know the group size

If you want groups of four, shuffle the list and place participants into groups of four until everyone is assigned. This is useful for workshops, discussion circles, and classroom activities.

How to make random grouping fair

Start with a fixed participant list. Each eligible person should normally appear once. If someone appears twice, that person has two entries and therefore a different chance of being assigned.

Decide exclusions before randomizing. For example, remove absent participants, people who opted out, or participants who are not eligible for that activity. Avoid changing assignments simply because the random result is inconvenient.

Random groups vs balanced teams

Random grouping and skill-balanced teams are different goals. Pure randomization does not know a participant's experience, skill level, position, or schedule.

If the activity needs balanced skill, you can randomize first and then apply a clearly stated balancing rule. For sports, you may need to consider positions or substitutes. For a general discussion activity, pure random grouping may be enough.

Classroom uses

Teachers can create random groups for projects, peer review, debates, reading activities, problem solving, quizzes, presentations, and classroom games. Pair this guide with our classroom random name generator guide when you also need to select individual students.

Workplace and workshop uses

Random groups can reduce repeated team combinations during brainstorming or training. A facilitator can shuffle participants before assigning discussion tables, breakout rooms, or project groups.

Sports and games

For casual sports, a random assignment can quickly divide players into teams. For more detail, read the random team generator for sports guide. If positions or competitive balance are important, use additional rules instead of assuming random groups will be equal in ability.

Can you keep two people apart?

Yes, but that changes the problem. If two participants cannot be in the same group, you are doing constrained random assignment. One practical approach is to randomize first and then resolve the constraint while keeping group sizes as even as possible.

State these constraints before the activity. Otherwise participants may reasonably question why the final result was changed.

Common mistakes

  • Leaving duplicate names in the list.
  • Changing the participant list after seeing the result.
  • Forgetting absent participants.
  • Assuming random automatically means skill-balanced.
  • Creating uneven groups when even sizes were possible.
  • Using one-at-a-time name picks when a full team assignment would be faster.

Simple checklist

  • Final participant list ready.
  • Eligibility confirmed.
  • Number of groups or group size decided.
  • Group-size rule decided.
  • Constraints identified in advance.
  • Randomization completed before unnecessary manual changes.

FAQs

How do I randomly assign people to groups?

Prepare the participant list, choose group rules, randomize, and distribute the names evenly.

How do I split 20 people into 4 groups?

Randomize the 20 names and create four groups of five.

Can random groups be equal in size?

Yes, when the participant count divides evenly. Otherwise keep the sizes as close as possible.

Can I prevent certain people from being together?

Yes, but that adds constraints to an otherwise random assignment.

Is random grouping the same as skill balancing?

No. Randomness does not account for skill unless you add a separate balancing process.

Can teachers use random groups?

Yes. It is useful for projects, discussions, games, peer review, and classroom activities.

What if I have an odd number of people?

Make groups as even as possible and decide how the extra participant will be handled.
